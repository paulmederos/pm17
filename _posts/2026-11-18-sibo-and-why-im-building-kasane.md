The short answer to why I'm building a health journal is my gut. The longer answer has a few parts: years with SIBO, a career spent around food, what I heard at a summit this month, and some timing that finally lines up.

## My gut, since I was a teenager

My stomach has bothered me since I was a teenager: bloating, pain, that kind of stuff.

In college I fell into CrossFit, and CrossFit led me to Paleo. I read Mark Sisson's *Primal Blueprint*, then Robb Wolf, then everyone else down that rabbit hole. I cut gluten and dairy and felt much better. It worked well enough that I ended up at Territory Foods, helping bring that way of eating to more people.

It helped. It didn't fix everything. Through my 20s it ebbed and flowed.

## A coworker went first

Around 2014 or 2015, one of my coworkers at Territory had something called SIBO: small intestinal bacterial overgrowth. It was pretty new back then, and plenty of GI doctors hadn't even heard of it.

I got to follow along as she worked it out, and it was eye-opening. I wasn't flaring much at the time, so I filed it away and kept going.

## It got worse after my daughter was born

I was 30 when my daughter was born, and my gut started acting up again. It got worse and worse over the next two years. Then the pandemic piled stress on top.

My primary care doctor and my GI weren't getting me anywhere, so I went looking for alternatives. I knew to do that because I'd watched my coworker do it. I found a functional medicine practitioner through Parsley Health—a former GI doctor who had switched over.

She helped right away. She suspected SIBO and sent me a breath test. It came back positive in April 2020: very high on hydrogen, with some methane too.

## Treatment took weeks, and getting better took a year

Treatment was rifaximin plus some Ayurvedic herbs, and it worked.

My digestion still wasn't good, though.

It took about a year of putting plain habits in place. I used to graze and snack all day, so I stopped. I shifted to more whole foods and more fiber, and I worked on my sleep. I started doing cardio consistently, which I'd never done, and that was a really big help. I stopped eating at 6 pm, which helped my sleep and my gut motility.

I kept a food and symptom journal through all of it. One entry from that July says, "Low FODMAP isn't going so well." Hah. I'd slipped back into bagels and cream cheese, and a few lines up I'd written that I fell off the tracking wagon. 😅

It wasn't until my mid-30s that I felt like I had SIBO under control.

## Where I am now

I still have digestive stuff, I still work with a functional GI doctor, and some of it is really hard to pin down. My best read is a combination: some histamine, some FODMAPs when I'm stressed, and some fermented foods.

The food journal has become my go-to. Depending on my symptoms and how I've been eating lately, I know what to shift. I rarely get more than one or two rough days in a row. Then I'm back to my baseline of good days.

## A career around food taught me the other half

I've spent most of my working life where health meets behavior: as an athlete, as a coach, and at Territory Foods, where we delivered meals to gym fridges and homes and tried to make healthy eating so easy that the habit just stuck.

During the pandemic I went deep on my own data. I got a full-body MRI and 150+ biomarker panels, and kept a spreadsheet going back a decade. It drove my health anxiety up. Knowing more didn't mean doing more. It meant worrying more.

My gut taught me the same thing from the other side. The breath test gave the problem a name. The year of plain habits is what made my days better. So I didn't want to build for health optimizers like me. I wanted to build for everybody else, like the parent at school pickup who knows something's off and never gets around to it.

## What I heard at Eudēmonia

I spent a few days at the Eudēmonia Summit earlier this month, and the gut kept coming up, the microbiome especially. So much of what's being discovered about whole-body health seems to start there.

I left thinking it isn't much of a stretch to say a lot of chronic disease starts in the gut. The research goes pretty far in that direction.

A [review in the BMJ](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6000740/) calls the microbiome "a virtual organ of the body": roughly 100 trillion microorganisms living in your gut. The same review says lower bacterial diversity "has been reproducibly observed" in people with inflammatory bowel disease, type 1 and type 2 diabetes, obesity, eczema, celiac disease, and stiffer arteries. [Another review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33803407/) puts 70 to 80% of the body's immune cells in the gut.

The careful part matters too. Most of this is association so far. That BMJ review says observational studies "are limited by the inability to measure causal relations." A link isn't proof that fixing your gut prevents any of these.

What stuck with me is simpler. The gut is connected to a lot, it responds to how you live, and improving it is hard to do alone. When I described Kasane to people there, as a journal that helps a family do that work, the room lit up.

## The size of it

I'm far from the only one with a gut like this. The [Rome Foundation's global study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32294476/) surveyed about 73,000 adults in 33 countries. About 4 in 10 of the people surveyed online met the criteria for at least one long-term gut condition, like IBS or chronic indigestion. In the household interviews it was about 2 in 10. Either way, that's a lot of people.

You can see them in search, too. In the US, "sibo symptoms" gets about 90,500 Google searches a month, and "stomach pain after eating" about 33,100. The searches with the word "gut" in them are small. The big ones name what hurts.

And the tools aren't working for them yet. When we read through reviews of the gut journals already out there, two complaints led: logging feels like a second job, and the analysis doesn't tell them much. Parents tracking for a kid are borrowing apps built for adults.

We also asked AI assistants the questions a parent would ask, like how to find which foods upset a child's stomach. They described a sensible method: write it down, change one thing, look back. They pointed to no source for it. That method needs a home.

## Why now

A food and symptom journal is already what clinicians ask for, and most people stop keeping one. I did too, at least once on the record.

What changed is the software. In early 2023, language models hit a tipping point. I started feeding them my labs, my history, and my questions, and for the first time something could actually pull it together. The same thing makes a journal you can keep. You type or say what happened, the way you'd text a friend, and it files each line at the right time.

That used to take a person. Now an app can do it.

## The parts of this that became Kasane

Looking back, three things did most of the work for me. They're the same three things [Kasane](https://www.kasanelife.com/) is built around.

**Writing it down.** Most of us see a doctor a few times a year. Meals, sleep, stress, and symptoms happen every day in between, and that's where my patterns showed up. Each provider had a slice of the picture, and it was up to me to connect the dots. The journal is where the dots live.

**Trying one small change.** When a rough day shows up now, I look back through the journal, pick one thing to shift, and watch what happens. Kasane calls that an experiment: one small change for a week or two, one quick question a day, and a look back at the end. Sometimes the answer is "that didn't help," and that's worth knowing too.

**Having someone in your corner.** I found my way to a diagnosis because a coworker shared hers. Answers come faster with company. So in Kasane your partner can see the change you're trying, each kid gets their own journal, and both parents can write in it. I'm a dad of two, and that last part matters a lot to me.

Kasane isn't only for SIBO. It's for anybody dealing with gut issues, and anybody paying a little more attention to what they eat and how they feel—plus the rest of life around it: stress, sleep, movement, the people you spend time with, your mental health.

## What I don't know yet

I don't have proof that families will keep a journal like this, or pay for one. I have my own story, a room's response, and search data. That's a reason to test, and it's a long way from an answer.

So we're testing it by hand. We're bringing in a first small group of families, half of them parents with their own gut issues and half with a kid's, and I'm onboarding each one myself. If that's your family, [I'd love to hear from you](https://www.kasanelife.com/first-families/). The number we're watching is good days: how many a family gets back while they try a change.

We're also not raising venture money for it. If families keep it and pay for it, a small, well-made journal is a real business. That's the bet.

## Why I talk about it

I knew to keep looking because a coworker shared what she was going through. That's a big reason I love sharing mine. The more people say what happened and what worked for them, the more relief there is for the next person.

I really hope Kasane helps way more people, and way more families, have many, many more good days. 🌿
